Framework 01 · Procurement · Tier 1 · Foundations

Procurement

The purpose, operating model and core processes of procurement in a single-site electronics OEM with global suppliers and customers.

At a Glance

Definition: Procurement is how the company gets the goods and services it needs from outside suppliers: the right quality, at the right total cost, on time, and with acceptable risk.

Relevance to our business: Most of the cost of our products is bought parts. We buy from suppliers around the world and sell mainly to customers in the US. What procurement does has a direct effect on our margin, on delivery to customers and on compliance.

  • Two kinds of spend: direct (goes into the product) and indirect (runs the business).
  • Every purchase follows the source-to-pay process: from need to payment in 8 steps.
  • Measure the value procurement creates, not the number of orders it processes.

First Principles

These are the reasons procurement works the way it does. They apply to every category and every supplier.

P-01

Procurement exists to create value, not to process transactions

Processing orders is necessary, but it is not the goal. The goal is to turn the money we spend outside into business results: lower total cost, reliable supply, good quality and new ideas from suppliers.

For us: measure procurement on total cost, supply continuity and quality, not on the number of purchase orders.
Root 2 · Total cost determines value
P-02

Influence is greatest before commitment

We have the most options when a need is first defined: the design, the specification and the choice of supplier are still open. After a part is designed in or a contract is signed, options shrink and every change costs money.

For us: procurement joins new product development at the design stage, not when the first order is needed.
Root 4 · Influence is greatest before commitment
P-03

Every sourcing decision is both a cost decision and a risk decision

A lower price often comes with more risk: a longer supply line, a weaker supplier or a single source. A decision that looks only at price is incomplete.

For us: record the risk position next to every important sourcing decision.
Root 3 · Risk = probability × impact
P-04

Visibility precedes control

We cannot manage spend, suppliers or risks that we cannot see. Clean data on what we buy, from whom and at what price is basic infrastructure, not a report.

For us: every purchase goes through the ERP system with the correct supplier, part and category.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-05

Distance turns lead time into cost and risk

Parts travel a long way to New Zealand, and our products travel a long way to US customers. Long and variable lead times mean more stock, more cash tied up and more exposure to freight problems.

For us: treat lead time as a commercial term, as important as price.
Root 5 · Distance and lead time drive stock, cash and risk
P-06

Relationships deliver what contracts cannot

A contract protects us when something goes wrong. A good relationship helps prevent problems: priority in shortages, early news of changes and faster problem solving.

For us: as a small buyer, being a customer that suppliers want to keep is one of our main sources of leverage.
Root 1 · Alternatives create leverage
P-07

Controls must be proportionate to value and risk

Rules protect the company. But rules that add delay without adding value push people to work around them. The effort should match the size of the spend and the risk.

For us: simple, fast paths for small routine purchases; real checks for large or risky commitments.
Root 8 · Effort proportional to value and risk

The Central Trade-off: Cost vs. Risk vs. Service

The business wants the lowest cost, the safest supply and the best service. These often conflict. The skill is to choose the balance on purpose, category by category, and to explain the choice.

Operating Model

Core idea: procurement sits between the supply market and the business. It turns needs into supply, and it manages spend by type, by process and by performance.

1 · Direct vs. Indirect Spend

Direct spend

Parts and materials that go into our products: semiconductors, passive components, PCBs, mechanical parts, cables and packaging. Direct spend follows the build plan. A shortage stops production and deliveries to customers. Priorities: supply continuity, total cost, quality and compliance of the part.

Indirect spend

Goods and services that run the business: IT, facilities, maintenance and spares, freight, professional services and travel. Often spread across many small suppliers. Priorities: simple buying channels, fewer suppliers and cost control.

4 · Measuring Savings

Cost reduction

Lower spend than the previous price for the same item and volume. Visible in the profit and loss statement. Examples: a lower price from a sourcing event, a cheaper approved alternate part.

Cost avoidance

A cost increase that we prevented, measured against a credible reference (for example a supplier's price increase request). Real value, but not visible in the profit and loss statement.

Other benefits

Improvements that do not reduce spend directly: shorter lead time, better quality, longer payment terms. Report them, but separately.

Not a saving

Savings against an unrealistic budget. Counting the same saving twice. Showing a part-year saving as a full year without saying so.

Common Failure Modes

The most common ways procurement fails. Each one violates a principle in tab 01.

01

Late involvement: procurement engaged after the decision

Engineering or the business chooses the part or supplier, then asks procurement to "raise the PO". No options are left.

Violates P-02: influence is greatest before commitment.
Warning sign: purchase orders for new parts with no sourcing record.
Make procurement a required participant in design reviews and budget approvals.
02

Uncontrolled off-contract spend

Money is spent outside agreed suppliers and channels. There is no price check, no volume leverage and more compliance risk.

Violates P-04: visibility precedes control.
Warning sign: many one-time suppliers and invoices without a purchase order.
Measure off-contract spend every month. Make the approved channel the easiest one to use.
03

Price focus at the expense of total cost and risk

The lowest price wins, and quality problems, delays and extra stock cost more later.

Violates P-03: every decision is a cost and a risk decision.
Warning sign: awards justified only by unit price.
Evaluate on total cost and risk. The commercial weight is typically 25–40% of the score, not 100%.
04

Contract signature treated as the finish line

Nobody owns the contract after signing. Performance and agreed value slowly erode.

Violates P-01: procurement exists to create value.
Warning sign: a contract without a named owner or review date.
Name the owner and the review schedule before signing.
05

Inflated savings reporting

Savings against an unrealistic budget, the same saving counted twice, or cost avoidance reported as reduction. Finance loses trust.

Violates P-01: procurement exists to create value.
Warning sign: reported savings that do not appear in product cost or the profit and loss statement.
Agree the method with finance once. Report reduction, avoidance and other benefits separately.
06

Heavy process on low-value spend

A full tender for a small purchase, or three quotes for routine items. People start to work around the process.

Violates P-07: controls must be proportionate.
Warning sign: long cycle times for small orders and complaints from the business.
Use catalogues, blanket orders and purchasing cards for small, repeat purchases.
07

Poor spend data

Wrong or missing supplier, part and category codes. Every analysis is only partly true.

Violates P-04: visibility precedes control.
Warning sign: a large "miscellaneous" category and duplicate supplier records.
Name an owner for the supplier master and the category list. Check them every quarter.
08

Lead time ignored as a commercial term

Suppliers are chosen on price while lead times grow. Stock, air freight and shortages increase.

Violates P-05: distance turns lead time into cost and risk.
Warning sign: ERP lead times differ from actual receipt times, and air freight is frequent.
Negotiate and track lead time and its variability in the same way as price.
Framework 02 · Category Management · Strategy · Reference

Category Management

Structuring spend into categories and managing each with a multi-year strategy: segmentation, sourcing and review.

At a Glance

Definition: A category is a group of spend that shares the same supply market, for example microcontrollers, PCBs or freight. Category management means you manage each category with one plan that covers several years.

Relevance to our business: We are a small buyer in global markets. A clear plan for each category is how we get good prices, secure supply and fewer surprises without the buying power of a large OEM.

  • Place each category in one of 4 segments (the Kraljic matrix). The segment tells you the strategy.
  • Follow 7 steps: define, spend and demand, needs, market, strategy, source, manage.
  • Review each plan at least once a year, and at once when a trigger happens (for example an end-of-life notice).

First Principles

These are the reasons category management works. If you understand them, you can build a good plan for any category, even one this guide does not cover.

P-01

Categories are defined by the supply market, not by internal structure

Parts that come from the same suppliers and have the same cost drivers should be managed together. Then you can see all your options, combine your volume and use one strategy. Groups based on our accounting codes or our product lines hide this.

For us: "Microcontrollers", "passive components", "PCBs" and "enclosures" are categories. "Product X parts" is not.
Root 1 · Alternatives create leverage
P-02

Segmentation determines strategy

A category with many suppliers and high spend needs competition. A category with only one supplier needs security of supply. Using the same approach for both either wastes money or creates risk. Segment first, then choose the strategy.

For us: use the Kraljic matrix (tab 02) before you decide how to source a category.
Root 8 · Effort proportional to value and risk
P-03

Requirements and market insight precede supplier engagement

Your options are widest before you contact suppliers. If you ask for quotes first, the suppliers define the choices, and you lose the chance to change the specification or find new sources.

For us: agree the requirements and approved alternates with engineering, and study the market, before any RFQ.
Root 4 · Influence is greatest before commitment
P-04

Total cost of ownership, not unit price, drives decisions

The price on the quote is only part of the cost. Quality problems, freight, stock, currency and switching also cost money. How deep you go should match the size of the spend: a full total cost model for big decisions, a simple check for small ones.

For us: a cheaper part with a longer lead time can cost more, because we must hold more stock in NZ.
Root 2 · Total cost determines value
P-05

Every strategy requires an explicit risk position

Lower cost and secure supply often pull in different directions. How much weight each one gets depends on the segment. What matters is that the choice is made on purpose and written down, not left to chance.

For us: if a category stays single-source, the plan says why, what protects us (stock, alternates) and who accepted the risk.
Root 3 · Risk = probability × impact
P-06

Customer attractiveness is a source of leverage

Suppliers decide who gets their best prices, their capacity in a shortage and early news about changes. They give these to customers who are important or easy to work with. When your volume is small, being a good customer is a big part of your leverage.

For us: good forecasts, fast decisions, on-time payment and design wins make us a customer that global suppliers want to keep.
Root 1 · Alternatives create leverage
P-07

Category strategy requires continuous review

Prices, lead times, suppliers, laws and our own products change. A plan that nobody updates slowly becomes wrong. Review each plan on a fixed date, and also when something important changes.

For us: an end-of-life notice, a big price or lead-time change, a new US tariff or a supplier event starts a review at once.
Root 9 · Conditions change continuously

The Central Trade-off: Cost Efficiency vs. Supply Security

Lower cost usually comes from competition and from putting volume with fewer suppliers. Security of supply usually comes from alternatives, stock and close relationships, which cost money. You cannot have the maximum of both. The Kraljic matrix (tab 02) shows which side to favour for each category.

Frameworks & Tools

Core idea: place each category by its profit impact and its supply risk, and the position tells you the strategy. Then check how the supplier sees you, because that tells you how much leverage you really have.

Examples for an electronics OEM (they are only examples, and a category can move over time): Strategic the main microcontroller or a custom display · Leverage standard PCBs, cables, sheet metal · Bottleneck a single-source connector or a part near end of life · Routine fasteners, labels, shop supplies.

2 · Supplier Preferencing Model: the supplier's perspective

Kraljic shows only the buyer's view. The supplier preferencing model (Steele & Court, 1996) shows the supplier's view. It uses two questions: how much is our business worth to the supplier, and how attractive are we as a customer (easy to work with, good payer, growing, a good name to have)?

Development · high attractiveness, low value

The supplier wants to grow the account. Good service, often good support. Use this to build the relationship early.

Core · high attractiveness, high value

The supplier protects you. Best service, capacity in shortages, early news. This is where you want your strategic suppliers to put you.

Nuisance · low attractiveness, low value

The supplier gives little attention. Slow answers, no priority in a shortage. Common for a small NZ buyer with a large global maker.

Exploitable · low attractiveness, high value

The supplier values the money but not the customer. Expect higher prices and less flexibility.

Implication for a Low-Volume Buyer

A category can be "strategic" for us while we are "nuisance" for the supplier. Then a plan that depends on buying power will fail. The answer is to buy through a distributor that values us, or to become more attractive: accurate forecasts, fast decisions, on-time payment and design wins.

Common Failure Modes

The most common ways category management fails. Each one breaks one of the principles in tab 01.

01

Premature sourcing: RFQ before market analysis

You do not know who the real suppliers are, what a fair price is or what options exist. The suppliers set the terms of the discussion.

Violates P-03: know your needs and the market first.
Warning sign: the RFQ list contains only the suppliers we already use.
Complete steps 3 and 4 before any RFQ. Add at least one supplier we have not used before.
02

Uniform effort across all categories

Category management takes time. Spending the same effort on routine items as on strategic parts leaves too little time for the categories that matter.

Violates P-02: different categories need different strategies.
Warning sign: the team has many plans, but no plan for the most important parts.
Segment first. Full plans for strategic and leverage categories, a short risk plan for bottleneck categories, and a simple buying method for routine ones.
03

Strategy built on leverage we do not have

The plan says "use competition" or "demand lower prices", but the supplier sees us as a small, low-value customer. The supplier does not respond, and the plan fails.

Violates P-06: suppliers also choose their customers.
Warning sign: slow or no answers to RFQs, and no priority when parts are short.
Check how the supplier sees us before choosing the strategy. Use a distributor or become a more attractive customer where needed.
04

Unmanaged single-source dependency

A category ends up with one supplier because nobody looked for another, not because someone decided it. When that supplier has a problem, there is no plan.

Violates P-05: every plan must state its risk position.
Warning sign: a part in the bill of materials has one approved maker and no alternate.
For each single source, write down why, what protects us (stock, alternates, redesign) and who accepted the risk.
05

Over-restrictive specifications

Engineering copies an old specification or names one exact part. This removes competition and turns a normal part into a bottleneck.

Violates P-03: agree the needs before talking to suppliers.
Warning sign: "only this part will work" with no test or reason written down.
Review specifications in step 3. Ask what the requirement is for, and approve alternates at the design stage.
06

Savings measured on price, not total cost

"We saved 10% on unit price" while freight, stock, quality problems or currency costs went up. The business can lose money while procurement reports a saving.

Violates P-04: decide on total cost.
Warning sign: savings are reported, but total cost in the product does not go down.
For large changes, report savings on total cost. Agree the method with finance once and use it every time.
07

Part or supplier changes outside change control

A new supplier or an alternate part is approved on price, but it has not been qualified, or the customer was not told. This can cause quality problems, stopped shipments or a breach of the customer contract.

Violates P-05: every plan must state its risk position.
Warning sign: purchase orders for a new part before engineering and quality have approved it.
Every change of part or supplier goes through engineering change control, qualification and, where the contract requires it, customer notification.
08

Static strategy: no review after approval

The plan is approved, the contract is signed and the team moves on. Two years later the market, the prices and our products have changed, but the plan has not.

Violates P-07: a category plan is a living plan.
Warning sign: the plan's date is more than 12 months old, or it does not mention the latest end-of-life notice or tariff change.
Name an owner, set a yearly review date and list the triggers that start an earlier review.
Framework 03 · Total Cost of Ownership · Tier 1 · Foundations

Total Cost of Ownership

Measuring the full cost of a sourcing decision over its life, not only the purchase price.

At a Glance

Definition: Total cost of ownership (TCO) is the sum of all costs of buying, receiving, holding, using and finally replacing a part or service, over the time we use it.

Relevance to our business: For a New Zealand OEM, many costs sit outside the price: freight over long distances, duty, stock held because of long lead times, quality problems, currency and the cost of switching suppliers.

  • Seven cost groups: acquisition, quality, logistics, inventory, operating, transition and risk.
  • The share of price depends on the category: often the largest part for electronic components, often only 20–40% for capital equipment.
  • Always compare at least two options over the same period.

First Principles

These principles explain why TCO changes decisions, and how to use it without false precision.

P-01

Price is only one part of cost

The quote shows the price. It does not show freight, duty, stock, quality failures or switching. How large the hidden part is depends on the category: smaller for standard components, larger for equipment and complex parts.

For us: for components, price is usually the largest share, but freight, stock and quality can still change which supplier is cheapest.
Root 2 · Total cost determines value
P-02

TCO is comparative, not absolute

One TCO number on its own means little. Its value comes from comparing options on the same basis: same volume, same period, same Incoterm.

For us: always model at least two options, for example the current supplier and the best alternative.
Root 2 · Total cost determines value
P-03

Risk must be expressed as cost

"This supplier is risky" is an opinion. "A 10% chance per year of a stop costing NZD 800,000 = NZD 80,000 expected cost per year" is a number we can compare.

For us: add expected risk cost as a line in every major TCO model.
Root 3 · Risk = probability × impact
P-04

The model is built before the decision, not after

A model built after the decision usually just justifies it. A model built before the decision shows what to evaluate and how to weight it.

For us: build the model before the RFQ and use it to set the evaluation criteria.
Root 4 · Influence is greatest before commitment
P-05

The time horizon must match the decision horizon

Qualification, tooling and switching costs are paid once but give benefits for years. A one-year view makes switching look too expensive, or hides costs that come later.

For us: use 3–5 years for significant categories, and the product life for parts that are designed in.
Root 2 · Total cost determines value
P-06

Lead time has a cost

A longer or less reliable lead time means more safety stock in NZ, more cash tied up, more risk of obsolescence and more air freight.

For us: always include the stock cost of lead time when comparing suppliers in different regions.
Root 5 · Distance and lead time drive stock, cash and risk
P-07

The model supports judgement; it does not replace it

A model is only as good as its assumptions. Show the assumptions, test the important ones and say how confident you are.

For us: mark every estimate and run a sensitivity check on the 3–5 biggest cost drivers.
Root 3 · Risk = probability × impact

The Central Trade-off: Precision vs. Speed of Decision

More detail can make a model more accurate, but it takes more time and can hide the main message. Model only at the level where more detail would change the decision.

Framework 04 · Negotiation · Tier 1 · Foundations

Negotiation

Reaching agreements that meet our objectives and that both sides are able to deliver.

At a Glance

Definition: Negotiation is the process of reaching an agreement with a supplier when each side has something the other side wants.

Relevance to our business: We are often a small customer negotiating with large global suppliers or distributors, in another culture and time zone. Good preparation and real alternatives matter more than tactics.

  • Set your Opening, Target and Walkaway before you start.
  • Your power comes from your best alternative (BATNA).
  • Trade across several issues, not only price.

First Principles

These principles apply to every negotiation, whatever the category, the supplier or the value.

P-01

Negotiating power comes from alternatives

If you can walk away, the other side must make a good offer. Your best option if there is no deal is called your BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement).

For us: many chips have only one maker. Build alternatives before you negotiate: approved alternate parts, extra stock, a second distributor.
Root 1 · Alternatives create leverage
P-02

Limits are set before the negotiation, not during it

Decide three points before you start: your Opening (ambitious), your Target (realistic) and your Walkaway (your limit). Limits set under pressure are usually worse.

For us: write the three points down and have them approved before an important call with a supplier.
Root 4 · Influence is greatest before commitment
P-03

Interests, not positions, create room for agreement

What a side asks for is its position. Why it asks is its interest. Two sides that disagree on price may both be happy with a different delivery plan or a longer contract.

For us: suppliers often value forecasts and volume certainty more than a small change in price.
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour
P-04

Agreement is only possible within the zone of possible agreement

If the supplier's lowest acceptable price is above our walkaway, there is no deal. The range between both limits is the ZOPA. Finding out early saves time for both sides.

For us: use should-cost and market data to estimate the supplier's limit.
Root 1 · Alternatives create leverage
P-05

Concessions are traded, never given

Every concession should get something back. Make each concession smaller than the last one, and do not make it too fast, so the other side sees you are near your limit.

For us: "we can commit to a 12-month forecast if you hold the price."
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour
P-06

Every negotiation is part of a repeated relationship

We will meet the same supplier again. A "win" that hurts the supplier comes back later as lower priority, less information and worse terms.

For us: as a small buyer, being a customer the supplier wants to keep is often worth more than the last 1% of price.
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour

The Central Trade-off: Value Creation vs. Value Claiming

Both sides can create value by trading issues they value differently, for example volume certainty for price, or a forecast for shorter lead time. Then the value is divided. Pushing too hard when dividing can destroy the value that was created, and the relationship.

Preparation Framework

Core idea: before any important negotiation, prepare three things: your limits, both sides' alternatives, and the list of issues that can be traded.

1 · The Position Stack

Set all three points before the first contact. They are internal and never shared with the supplier.

Opening

Your first offer. Ambitious, but you can justify it with data. More ambitious than your target, so you have room to move.

Target

The realistic, good result you aim for. Based on market data, should-cost and past deals.

Walkaway

Your limit. Beyond this point your BATNA is better than the deal. Set and approved before the negotiation, and not changed during it without new approval.

2 · BATNA and ZOPA

Our BATNA

List every alternative: another qualified supplier, an alternate part, another distributor, stock, a redesign or a delay. Develop the best one until it is real. For sole-source parts our BATNA can be weak, so improve it before the negotiation.

Their BATNA

Their other customers, how full their capacity is, and how important we are to them. A supplier with spare capacity has a weak BATNA. A sole source in a shortage has a strong one.

ZOPA

The range between our walkaway and their walkaway. If the ranges do not overlap, there is no deal. Neither side knows the other's limit exactly; good preparation gives a better estimate.

Improving our position

The most effective way to improve an outcome is to improve our BATNA before we start: qualify an alternate, build stock or bring in a second distributor.

4 · Negotiation Styles

Collaborative

Focus on shared interests and joint problem solving. Best for strategic suppliers and long-term agreements.

With suppliers in other countries: confirm everything in writing, agree the agenda in advance, check who has authority, and allow for different ways of saying "no".

Most negotiation failures are preparation failures, not tactical ones. Each one violates a principle in tab 01.

Without a limit set in advance, any deal feels acceptable under pressure.

Violates P-02: limits are set before the negotiation.

02

A modest opening leaves no room to move. The supplier expects movement and we have none to give.

Violates P-02: limits are set before the negotiation.
Warning sign: the first offer equals the target.
Open more ambitiously than the target, with data to support it, so there is room for two or three steps.

Weak alternative not improved before negotiating

We negotiate a sole-source part with no alternate and no stock, and the supplier knows it.

Warning sign: no alternate part or source in the plan.

Price as the only issue

Too much talking

Negotiation treated as a fight

08

No debrief

What we learned about the supplier is lost. The next negotiation starts from zero.

Violates P-06: every negotiation is part of a repeated relationship.
Warning sign: no written notes after important negotiations.
Debrief within 48 hours and keep a negotiation file per supplier.
Framework 05 · Contract Management · Tier 1 · Foundations

Contract Management

Turning a signed contract into the value that was agreed, and protecting the company during the life of the contract.

At a Glance

Definition: Contract management is the work after signing: making sure both sides do what they agreed, managing changes, and deciding what happens at renewal or exit.

Relevance to our business: We buy from global suppliers under different laws, and we sell to US customers under their terms. Our supplier contracts must support what we promise our customers.

  • Six parts of every good contract: commercial, performance, risk, change, exit, governance.
  • Lifecycle: register, hand over, operate, change, renew, exit.
  • Value is lost after signing: World Commerce & Contracting estimated an average loss of about 11% of contract value (2025).

First Principles

These principles explain why contracts lose value after signing, and what protects that value.

P-01

Value is realised after signature, not at signature

A contract only promises value. Price creep, missed rebates, unclaimed penalties and unmanaged changes reduce it. Research by World Commerce & Contracting (2025) found an average loss of about 11% of contract value after signing.

For us: every important contract has a named owner and a review calendar.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-02

Supplier commitments must support customer commitments

What we promise our customers (warranty, delivery, compliance, change notice) depends on what our suppliers promise us. Any gap between the two is a risk we carry alone.

For us: check warranty, liability, change notification and compliance terms against our main US customer contracts.
Root 3 · Risk = probability × impact
P-03

Clarity at signature prevents disputes later

Vague words become disputes in year two. Measures, acceptance criteria and scope must be defined precisely.

For us: define "on time" as the date at our dock in NZ, not the ship date, and write defect limits and notice periods as numbers.
Root 4 · Influence is greatest before commitment
P-04

Obligations run in both directions

The buyer also has duties: forecasts, payment, clear specifications. When the buyer fails, the supplier's first defence is our own failure.

For us: track our forecast accuracy and payment performance as carefully as supplier performance.
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour
P-05

Change is certain; how it is managed decides the outcome

No contract survives unchanged. Changes made formally (written, priced, approved) protect us. Informal changes lead to disputes.

For us: no change to price, scope, specification or dates without a written change.
Root 9 · Conditions change continuously
P-06

Exit terms are negotiated at the start

We have the most leverage before signing. Once a relationship is going badly, the supplier has little reason to help us leave.

For us: agree tooling ownership, last-time-buy rights, end-of-life notice and transition support in the original contract.
Root 4 · Influence is greatest before commitment
P-07

Governing law determines how a contract is enforced

In international contracts, the chosen law and the place of dispute resolution decide how rights are enforced. For international sales of goods, the UN Convention (CISG) may apply by default unless the contract excludes it.

For us: state the governing law and dispute method in every international contract, and check the CISG position with our lawyer.
Root 3 · Risk = probability × impact

The Central Trade-off: Control vs. Flexibility

Tight terms protect us but make changes slow and expensive. Loose terms allow flexibility but leave us exposed. Use tight terms where risk is high, and flexible terms where needs change often.

Supplier Risk Management

Identifying, assessing and reducing risks that start in the supply base, before they stop production or block deliveries.

At a Glance

Definition: Supplier risk management means finding the risks in our supply base, measuring them, and deciding what to do about each one.

Relevance to our business: Our supply chain is long and global, many parts have only one maker, and our main market, the US, has strict import rules. A problem at one supplier can stop our line or block our shipments. (Supply Resilience covers how fast we recover; this topic covers finding and reducing risk.)

  • Eight risk types, from disruption to compliance.
  • Score each risk: probability × impact.
  • Start with critical and single-source parts.

First Principles

These principles explain how supply risk behaves and where risk management effort pays off.

P-01

Risk that is not quantified cannot be prioritised

Saying "this supplier is risky" creates awareness but not action. Probability and impact turn a concern into a number we can rank and compare with the cost of protection.

For us: score every risk on the same 1–5 scales, with written definitions.
Root 3 · Risk = probability × impact
P-02

Scrutiny should match criticality

A problem with a sole-source part has much bigger consequences than a problem with a part that has twelve suppliers. Spread effort by importance, not evenly.

For us: start with single-source parts, long-lead parts and parts on our best-selling products.
Root 8 · Effort proportional to value and risk
P-03

Most disruptions start below tier 1

A direct supplier can look strong while its own suppliers are fragile. A single wafer fab, substrate maker or chemical plant can stop many "different" suppliers at once.

For us: for critical parts, ask where the die, substrate or key material comes from.

P-04

Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-05

Early warning is worth more than fast reaction

Root 4 · Influence is greatest before commitment
P-06

Protection has a cost, and so does accepted risk

For us: every accepted risk has a written reason and an owner.

P-07

For us: with about 80% of sales to the US, check origin, forced-labour exposure and export control status for every critical part.

More protection costs more: stock, second sources, audits and monitoring. Spend where the expected loss is highest, and accept small risks on purpose.

Framework 07 · Spend Analysis · Tier 2 · Strategic Tools

Spend Analysis

Turning purchase data into a clear view of what we buy, from whom and at what cost, and where the opportunities and risks are.

At a Glance

Definition: Spend analysis is the regular collection, cleaning and classification of purchase data, so that we can see patterns, opportunities and risks.

Relevance to our business: We buy in several currencies (mainly USD and NZD) from suppliers around the world. Direct spend follows our bill of materials and build plan. Without clean data, every category plan and negotiation starts from guesses.

  • Three dimensions: category, supplier, and product line or cost centre.
  • Seven steps: extract, consolidate, cleanse, classify, enrich, analyse, act.
  • Refresh every quarter or half-year, with the same rules each time.

First Principles

These principles explain why spend data is the base of every other procurement decision.

P-01

Visibility precedes control

We can only use leverage on spend we can see. Spend that is not analysed is not managed: prices go unchecked and opportunities stay hidden.

For us: include all spend, not only purchase-order spend: invoices without a PO and card spend too.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-02

Data quality limits every result

Wrong codes, different spellings of the same supplier and missing categories give wrong answers. Cleaning and classifying the data is the main work, not a preparation step.

For us: one supplier record per legal entity, and one reporting currency (NZD) with the rate used written down.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-03

Classification should serve decisions

A perfect category tree that produces no decisions has no value. Classify at the level where different categories need different strategies.

For us: three to five levels are almost always enough.
Root 8 · Effort proportional to value and risk
P-04

Direct spend must be read together with the bill of materials

For production parts, spend follows the build plan. To understand it, link spend to part numbers, products and future demand, not only to past invoices.

For us: connect spend to the bill of materials, so we can see which products and customers depend on which suppliers.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-05

Supplier count must balance leverage and resilience

Too many small suppliers waste time and spread volume. Too few create dependency. The right number is different for each category.

For us: reduce the indirect tail, but keep qualified alternates for direct parts.
Root 1 · Alternatives create leverage
P-06

Off-contract spend signals a process problem

When people buy outside agreed channels, the channel often does not meet their need. It is a signal to investigate, not only a rule to enforce.

For us: ask why off-contract spend happens before adding new rules.
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour
P-07

Spend analysis is a recurring process, not a project

One analysis is a snapshot. Repeated with the same rules, it shows trends: price drift, new dependencies and growing categories.

For us: refresh every quarter or half-year and keep the rules the same.
Root 9 · Conditions change continuously

The Central Trade-off: Completeness vs. Speed

Perfect classification takes months. Most decisions only need most of the spend classified correctly. 90% classified on time is worth more than 97% classified too late.

The Spend Cube

Core idea: look at spend through three dimensions at the same time. Combining them shows patterns that one dimension alone cannot show.

What we buy, in a hierarchy: category → sub-category → part family. Can follow a standard (for example UNSPSC) or our own structure.

For what: product line or cost centre

Which product, customer program or cost centre the spend supports. Shows which products depend on which suppliers.

Which few suppliers or categories make up most of the spend? High concentration = leverage or dependency. A long tail = room to simplify.

Framework 08 · Should-Cost Modelling · Tier 2 · Strategic Tools

Should-Cost Modelling

Estimating what a part should cost to make, from its materials, processes and a fair margin, independent of any supplier quote.

At a Glance

Definition: A should-cost model estimates what a part or service should cost, built from its materials, labour, overhead, tooling, freight and a fair margin.

Relevance to our business: It works best for PCBs, cable assemblies, sheet metal, machined and moulded parts, and assemblies we understand well. It is less useful for standard semiconductors, where the price depends on the maker's market position; there, use market price benchmarks instead.

  • Six cost layers: materials, labour, overhead, tooling, freight, margin.
  • Build it before the RFQ, not after.
  • Use it as a basis for discussion with the supplier, not as a demand.

First Principles

These principles explain what a should-cost model can and cannot tell you.

P-01

Every cost can be broken down into its drivers

Any part cost can be split into materials, labour, overhead, tooling, freight and margin. None of these is unknowable. A supplier that will not explain its costs is taking a negotiating position.

For us: ask suppliers of custom parts for a cost breakdown in the RFQ.
Root 2 · Total cost determines value
P-02

The model is independent of the supplier's quote

The model is built from outside data: material prices, labour rates, overhead benchmarks and freight rates. Its value comes from this independence. A large gap between model and quote needs an explanation.

For us: build the model before you open the quotes.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-03

Should-cost is a fair price, not a floor

The model includes a fair margin for the supplier. So it estimates a fair price, not the lowest price the supplier could survive. Use it as the reference for your target, not as a demand.

For us: a quote close to should-cost is a fair offer. A quote far above it needs explaining.
Root 2 · Total cost determines value
P-04

The model is most useful before the RFQ

Built early, the model shapes the specification, the target price and the choice of suppliers. Built late, it can only react to quotes.

For us: build the model while the specification can still change.
Root 4 · Influence is greatest before commitment
P-05

Engineering knowledge is required

Process steps, cycle times and material use need technical knowledge. Without it, the supplier can easily show that the model is wrong.

For us: our engineers know our designs and processes. Involve them in every model.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-06

A gap between model and quote is information, not proof

A quote 40% above the model may include costs or risks the model misses, or excess margin. Both are useful to know.

For us: share the model and ask the supplier where our assumptions are wrong.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-07

Should-cost fits some categories better than others

The method works where the cost is driven by materials and processes. It works less well where price is driven by technology, brand or scarcity.

For us: use should-cost for custom and mechanical parts; use market benchmarks for standard semiconductors.
Root 8 · Effort proportional to value and risk

The Central Trade-off: Accuracy vs. Effort

A rough model (±15%) can be built in days. A precise model (±5%) can take weeks. Choose the level of accuracy by the size of the spend and the decision.

Cost Architecture

Core idea: six layers of cost, from materials to margin. Build each layer from outside data and our own engineering knowledge.

1 · Six Cost Layers (click to expand)

◈
Direct Materials
Usually the largest layer for manufactured parts
+

Build from the bill of materials: every component and raw material, at market price, plus a yield or scrap factor, plus the effect of minimum order quantities.

Data sources: London Metal Exchange (metals), chemical and polymer price services, distributor price lists (for example Digi-Key, Mouser, Arrow) for electronic parts, and industry price trackers for memory and semiconductors.

Watch for: material substitutions without notice, and parts near end of life.

⚒
Direct Labour
Process time × fully loaded labour rate
+

Process time comes from engineering estimates, time studies or similar parts. Labour rates differ greatly by country; the same assembly can cost many times more in one country than in another.

Data sources: ILO wage data, regional salary surveys, published contract manufacturer rates.

Watch for: skill premiums, shift and overtime premiums, and automation that moves cost from labour to machines.

⚙
Manufacturing Overhead
Factory costs applied as a burden rate
+

Facility, utilities, depreciation, maintenance, quality and supervision. Usually applied as a percentage of labour cost or per machine hour.

Indicative burden rates (vs. direct labour): electronics contract manufacturing 80–140%, precision machining 150–250%, high-volume plastics 60–100%. These vary with automation and utilisation.

Watch for: high overhead caused by low utilisation. That is the supplier's problem to fix, not ours to pay for.

⚑

One-off engineering, tooling and qualification
+

Design, moulds, fixtures, test equipment and first-article qualification. Paid once, or spread over the contract volume.

Key questions: who owns the tooling? Who pays to maintain it? What if the volume is lower than planned?

Note: buyer-owned tooling can move to another supplier. Supplier-owned tooling creates lock-in.

⇢
Freight and Logistics
Getting the part to our dock
+

Freight, insurance, duty, packaging and handling. Often underestimated for heavy or bulky parts, and for long routes like ours.

Data sources: container and air freight rate indices, freight forwarder quotes, NZ Customs tariff.

Watch for: delivered (DDP) prices that hide a freight margin.

%
SG&A and Margin
The supplier's overhead and a fair profit
+

Selling, general and administrative costs (indicatively 5–15% of revenue for manufacturers) plus a profit margin (indicatively 5–15%, higher for specialised or IP-rich suppliers).

Data sources: annual reports of listed competitors, industry financial benchmarks.

Note: a supplier with high margins in a competitive category either adds real value or has not faced enough competition.

Low fit: standard semiconductors and other branded components, where price is set by the market; use distributor prices, price history and market reports instead.

Supplier Selection & Evaluation

Selecting the supplier best able to meet our requirements, with a decision that is fair, documented and defensible.

At a Glance

Definition: Supplier selection is the structured process of finding, checking and choosing a supplier for a part, category or service.

Relevance to our business: New suppliers are often far away, site visits cost time and money, and a new part may need qualification and customer approval. Suppliers of parts for US-bound products must also pass compliance checks.

  • Fix criteria and weights before reading any offer.
  • Pass/fail requirements first, then scoring.
  • Eight steps, from requirements to onboarding.

First Principles

These principles explain what makes a selection decision good and defensible.

P-01

Supplier selection creates long-term dependency

A new supplier becomes part of our products for years. A wrong choice is slow and expensive to reverse. Time spent on a careful selection is repaid many times.

For us: the more critical the part, the deeper the evaluation.
Root 4 · Influence is greatest before commitment
P-02

Criteria are fixed before offers are read

Criteria set after reading offers are shaped by those offers, even without intent. Fixing them first is fair to suppliers and makes the decision defensible.

For us: approve criteria and weights before the RFQ goes out.
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour
P-03

Price is one criterion among several

The cheapest supplier is often not the cheapest in total: quality, delivery, freight, stock and risk also cost money.

For us: score the commercial part on total cost, typically 25–40% of the total weight.
Root 2 · Total cost determines value
P-04

Pass/fail requirements come before scoring

Some requirements are not negotiable: certifications, compliance, financial stability. A supplier that fails one is out, however good the price.

For us: include export-control, sanctions and forced-labour checks as pass/fail for US-bound products.
Root 8 · Effort proportional to value and risk
P-05

Verification beats declaration

A questionnaire shows what the supplier wants us to believe. A site visit, a financial check and reference calls show what is true.

For us: for strategic parts, verify on site or through a trusted third-party audit.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-06

Suppliers also decide whether to compete for our business

Preparing an offer costs suppliers time. For a small buyer, a long, unclear process means good suppliers do not bid.

For us: short-list 3–5 suppliers, give clear volumes and specifications, and keep the process simple.

P-07

The Central Trade-off: Thoroughness vs. Speed and Cost

A deeper evaluation reduces risk but takes time and money on both sides. Match the depth of the evaluation to the importance and risk of the part.

Core idea: a weighted scorecard with six areas, preceded by pass/fail requirements and supported by the right RFx document.

Anchored 1–5 scale

A cross-functional team sets the weights; the sourcing authority approves them. Individual evaluators do not change them.

Certifications, minimum financial stability, compliance (export control, sanctions, forced labour), capacity. Fail one = out.

Framework 10 · Supplier Relationship Management · Tier 2 · Strategic Tools

Supplier Relationship Management

Investing in a small number of supplier relationships where joint work creates value that normal supplier management cannot.

At a Glance

Definition: Supplier relationship management (SRM) is a deliberate programme of closer work with a few strategically important suppliers, to create value for both sides.

Relevance to our business: Our team is small, so we can only invest deeply in a few relationships, typically 3–8. For a small buyer, a strong relationship is also how we get priority in shortages and early news about changes.

  • Four supplier tiers; SRM is for tier 1 only.
  • Three review levels: monthly, quarterly, yearly.
  • Scorecards are the minimum; joint projects create the real value.

First Principles

These principles explain why SRM works for a few suppliers and fails when spread too widely.

P-01

SRM is selective by design

SRM means spending more time and senior attention on a few suppliers. Applied to all suppliers, it becomes normal supplier management and the extra value disappears.

For us: size the strategic tier to what our team can really support, typically 3–8 suppliers.
Root 8 · Effort proportional to value and risk
P-02

Value must flow in both directions

A programme that only asks suppliers for more will fail. Strategic suppliers notice one-sided relationships and respond with less openness and less effort.

For us: define what we offer: forecasts, fast payment, early design involvement, volume commitment.
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour
P-03

The relationship belongs to the organisation, not to one person

If the relationship depends on one person, it ends when that person leaves. It must be built into regular meetings, records and several contacts on each side.

For us: at least two people from each side know each strategic supplier relationship.
Root 9 · Conditions change continuously
P-04

SRM goes beyond contract compliance

Contract management makes sure both sides do what was agreed. SRM builds the trust and joint work that let both sides do more than the contract requires.

For us: separate contract reviews from strategic discussions.
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour
P-05

Senior sponsorship signals importance

When senior managers on both sides meet regularly, problems are solved faster and the supplier gives the relationship more priority.

For us: assign a senior sponsor for each tier 1 supplier.
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour
P-06

Knowing the supplier's strategy protects our supply

A supplier's roadmap, investments and end-of-life plans affect our products. A good relationship gives this information early.

For us: ask strategic suppliers for their product roadmap and end-of-life plans every year.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-07

Our attractiveness as a customer is part of the relationship

Suppliers give their best service to customers they value. Our forecast accuracy, payment, stable specifications and fairness all count.

For us: ask strategic suppliers each year how they rate us as a customer.
Root 1 · Alternatives create leverage

The Central Trade-off: Depth vs. Breadth

Deep relationships with a few suppliers create the most value, but they cost time and attention. Spreading effort across many suppliers produces little value with any of them.

Five scorecard dimensions

Quality (defects, first-article pass rate), delivery (on time, lead time), commercial (invoice accuracy, savings), responsiveness (issue resolution) and innovation (ideas, roadmap engagement).

Scoring discipline

Score each dimension on a defined 1–5 scale. A composite below an agreed threshold (for example 3.0) starts an improvement plan. A high score can earn more volume or preferred status.

Relationship health check

Does the supplier raise problems early? Share market information? Are meetings useful? These signals predict the future of the relationship better than any KPI.

The supplier's view of us

Ask strategic suppliers to rate us: forecast accuracy, payment, specification stability, communication and fairness. Act on what they say.

4 · Joint Value Creation

Design for manufacture

Involve the supplier early in new products to improve cost and producibility.

Process improvement

Joint projects to reduce defects, stock or lead time.

Technology roadmap

Plan part choices around the supplier's roadmap and end-of-life plans.

Demand visibility

Share a rolling 12–18 month forecast so the supplier can plan capacity.

Supply resilience

Agree buffer stock, allocation priority and joint risk mapping.

Sustainability

Joint work on emissions, materials and compliance data.

Framework 11 · Supply Market Analysis · Tier 2 · Strategic Tools

Supply Market Analysis

Understanding the external forces, suppliers and trends that decide what is possible in a category.

At a Glance

Definition: Supply market analysis is the structured study of a supply market: who the suppliers are, how power is distributed, what drives cost and capacity, and where the market is going.

Relevance to our business: We buy in global markets where we are a small customer. Knowing how a market works, and how suppliers see us, tells us which strategies are realistic. For electronics, the product lifecycle and capacity cycles are especially important.

  • Five lenses: Porter's Five Forces, PESTLE, capacity, supplier landscape, price trends.
  • Seven steps from scope to strategy.
  • Refresh at least yearly for strategic categories, and after any major market event.

First Principles

These principles explain why market knowledge sets the limits of every category strategy.

P-01

The market sets the limits of strategy

A strategy that ignores the market fails at execution. The market decides whether competition is possible, whether a second source exists and whether this is a good time to negotiate.

For us: complete the market analysis before building a category plan.
Root 1 · Alternatives create leverage
P-02

Supplier economics explain supplier behaviour

A supplier with thin margins and full capacity behaves differently from one with good margins and spare capacity. Their costs and position explain their offers.

For us: check the supplier's capacity use and financial results before a negotiation.
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour
P-03

Concentration shapes power and risk

A market with two or three suppliers is very different from one with twenty. High concentration gives suppliers power and makes the market fragile.

For us: measure concentration with the number of suppliers or the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI).
Root 1 · Alternatives create leverage
P-04

External cost drivers must be separated from margin

Material, energy, labour and currency changes affect supplier costs whatever the supplier does. Price increases beyond these drivers are margin, not cost.

For us: test every price increase against the relevant indices, weighted by their share of the part cost.
Root 2 · Total cost determines value
P-05

The technology lifecycle drives electronics supply

Parts move from new to mature to end of life. Older technologies lose capacity and suppliers; new ones may offer lower cost if you move early.

For us: track the lifecycle stage of every critical part.
Root 9 · Conditions change continuously
P-06

Capacity utilisation predicts price and lead time

When suppliers are nearly full, lead times grow and prices rise. When they have spare capacity, they compete for volume. Timing matters.

For us: run sourcing events when the market has spare capacity, and secure supply before it tightens.
Root 9 · Conditions change continuously
P-07

Market analysis must be refreshed

A market can change a lot in 12–18 months: prices, supplier consolidation, trade rules, technology. Old analysis gives false confidence.

For us: refresh before every major sourcing event and at least once a year for strategic categories.
Root 9 · Conditions change continuously

The Central Trade-off: Depth of Analysis vs. Timeliness

A deep analysis gives better insight but takes time. Analysis that arrives after the strategy is decided has no value. Finish the analysis 3–6 months before a major sourcing event.

3 · Capacity, Landscape and Price

Capacity utilisation

Rule of thumb: above about 85%, supply tightens, lead times grow and prices rise; below about 75%, suppliers compete for volume. Watch utilisation and order backlogs for key markets.

Supplier landscape and concentration

Map all suppliers globally, regionally and those qualified to our standards. HHI = sum of squared market shares (in %). The 2023 US Merger Guidelines treat an HHI above 1,800 as highly concentrated (older guidelines used 2,500).

Price trend analysis

Track prices against relevant indices. Separate index-driven changes from changes above the index. Example: if a part is 15% copper and copper rises 20%, the justified effect is about 3%, not 20%.

Technology roadmap position

Where is the part in its lifecycle? Which suppliers invest in the next generation? A sole source that is not investing is a future risk.

Distribution Channels in Electronics

Most components reach us through franchised (authorised) distributors, not directly from the maker. Distributors hold stock, combine small customers' volume and handle design registration. Independent brokers can help in shortages, but they carry a high counterfeit risk. Understanding the channel is part of understanding the market.

Inventory & Planning Interface

How demand, inventory policy and supplier lead times interact to decide what we buy, when and in what quantity.

At a Glance

Definition: Inventory is stock held to cover the gap between when we need a part and when a supplier can deliver it. Planning decides how much stock to hold and when to order.

Relevance to our business: Our parts come from far away with long lead times, and many electronic parts become obsolete quickly. Every inventory decision balances service to our customers against cash tied up in stock and the risk of scrap.

  • Lead time is the main driver of how much stock we need.
  • Safety stock = service factor × demand variation × √lead time.
  • Holding cost is typically 20–30% of stock value per year.

First Principles

These principles explain why inventory exists and what really drives the amount we need.

P-01

Inventory exists to decouple supply from demand

Suppliers cannot deliver instantly, demand is not perfectly predictable and production runs in batches. Every unit of stock covers one of these gaps. Knowing which gap a stock covers tells us how much we need.

For us: separate cycle stock, safety stock and strategic buffer stock in our reports.
Root 5 · Distance and lead time drive stock, cash and risk
P-02

Service level is a decision, not an outcome

The chance of running out that we accept is a business choice. A 99% service level needs much more safety stock than 95%. The level should depend on how critical the part is.

For us: set service levels by item class and by the cost of a stock-out, not one level for everything.
Root 8 · Effort proportional to value and risk
P-03

Lead time is the dominant planning variable

Reorder points, safety stock, obsolescence exposure and forecast error all grow with lead time. Cutting lead time from 12 to 6 weeks halves the time we are exposed and can free up cash.

For us: negotiate lead time and its reliability like price.
Root 5 · Distance and lead time drive stock, cash and risk
P-04

Variability costs more than volume

Stable demand is cheap to serve. Demand that jumps up and down needs safety stock, expediting and spare capacity across the chain. Reducing variability often saves more than a lower unit price.

For us: share forecasts and smooth our orders to suppliers.

P-05

Order quantities are inventory decisions

A minimum order quantity or a price break moves stock from the supplier's books to ours. The price saving must be weighed against holding cost, obsolescence and cash.

For us: a 5% price break that forces 9 months of stock is often a loss. Always check the numbers.
Root 2 · Total cost determines value
P-06

Obsolescence is a primary electronics inventory risk

Component lifecycles are often shorter than product lifecycles. Excess stock can lose all its value after an engineering change or an end-of-life notice.

For us: last-time-buy decisions are made by engineering, product, finance and procurement together.

P-07

Forecast error must be measured and planned for

The forecast will be wrong; the question is by how much. Measure the error (MAPE, bias) and size buffers from it.

For us: measure forecast error by item class and use it to set safety stock.

The Central Trade-off: Service vs. Stock

Higher service levels need more stock, which ties up cash and increases obsolescence risk. Lower stock frees cash but increases the risk of stopping production or missing customer deliveries. Decide the balance item by item.

Core Mechanics

Core idea: a few simple models make the trade-offs visible. Their value is in showing what each choice costs, not in decimal precision.

1 · Planning Models (click to expand)

∑
Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)
EOQ = √(2DS ÷ H)
+

D = annual demand, S = fixed cost per order, H = holding cost per unit per year. EOQ is the order size with the lowest total ordering and holding cost.

Use it for: a starting point for order size, and to show the cost of a supplier's minimum order quantity.

Limits: it assumes stable demand and no price breaks. Adjust for MOQs, pack sizes and shelf life.

σ
Safety Stock and Service Level
SS = Z × σd × √LT
+

Z = service factor (1.65 for 95%, 2.33 for 99%), σd = standard deviation of demand per period, LT = lead time in periods. Extended versions also include lead-time variability, which often matters more.

Key point: moving from 95% to 99% service needs about 40% more safety stock; 99.9% needs almost twice as much.

For procurement: reducing lead-time variability cuts safety stock directly. Measure delivery consistency, not only on-time delivery.

↺
Reorder Point (ROP)
ROP = average demand × lead time + safety stock
+

When the stock position (on hand + on order − allocated) falls to the reorder point, a new order is triggered.

Watch: use stock position, not stock on hand, or you will order twice. Update the reorder point immediately when a lead time changes.

▦
ABC / XYZ Segmentation
Value × variability
+

ABC ranks items by annual value (A ≈ top 80% of value, B next 15%, C last 5%). XYZ ranks by demand variability (X stable, Y seasonal or trending, Z erratic).

Policies: AX = automated statistical replenishment and long-term agreements with releases. AZ = human planning, flexibility clauses, supplier buffers. CX = min/max, kanban or supplier-managed stock. CZ = consider buying to order only.

⚖
MOQ and Price-Break Economics
Saving vs. extra holding cost
+

Compare the price saving on annual volume with the extra holding cost of the larger order: extra average stock × holding rate (typically 20–30% per year). Include obsolescence risk for parts with short lifecycles.

Rule of thumb: if a price break forces more than 3–4 extra months of stock for a saving below 5%, it usually does not pay. Counter-offer: the break price for a committed annual volume with scheduled releases.

⇄
VMI, Consignment and Buffer Agreements
Who holds and funds the stock
+

Vendor-managed inventory (VMI): the supplier plans and refills stock at our site within agreed limits. Consignment: stock at our site stays the supplier's until we use it. Buffer agreement: the supplier holds agreed stock for us, paid for by a volume commitment or a fee.

The honest trade: all three move cost or risk to the supplier, who will price it in. It only creates real savings when the supplier can hold the stock more cheaply than we can.

⏏
Last-Time Buy (LTB)
The final purchase before end of life
+

The quantity must cover remaining production, service and warranty needs for the whole support period, often 5–10 years for industrial electronics. Build it from the production forecast, expected field failures, yield loss and a safety margin.

Before buying: check alternatives: compatible replacement parts, stock at distributors, die banking or a redesign. An LTB is a long bet with no value if it is wrong; it needs sign-off from engineering, product, finance and procurement.

Framework 13 · Supply Chain Resilience · Tier 3 · Operational Depth

Supply Chain Resilience

Designing a supply chain that can absorb, adapt to and recover from disruption, before a disruption happens.

At a Glance

Definition: Resilience is the ability of the supply chain to keep serving customers during a disruption and to recover quickly afterwards. Risk management asks "what could go wrong?"; resilience asks "how fast do we recover?".

Relevance to our business: We depend on long, thin supply routes to NZ, many single-maker parts and one factory site. A disruption at a supplier, in freight or at our own site can stop deliveries to our customers.

  • Compare time to survive (TTS) with time to recover (TTR) for each critical node.
  • Seven levers, from second sources to site continuity.
  • Resilience must be tested regularly or it disappears.

First Principles

These principles explain how to build recovery capability where it matters most.

P-01

Efficiency and resilience must be traded off deliberately

Lean supply chains with little stock and few suppliers are efficient but fragile. Neither extreme is right. The trade-off should be decided part by part, not left to chance.

For us: decide the resilience level for each critical part and write it down.
Root 3 · Risk = probability × impact
P-02

Resilience requires multi-tier visibility

Disruptions often start at tier 2 or deeper: a wafer fab, a substrate maker, a chemical plant. Several "different" suppliers can depend on the same source.

For us: map critical parts until the supply paths really separate.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control
P-03

Time to survive vs. time to recover defines exposure

For each critical node, ask: if it fails, how long until supply returns (TTR), and how long can we continue without it (TTS)? If TTR is longer than TTS, the exposure is real and measurable (Simchi-Levi et al., 2014).

For us: calculate TTR and TTS for the top 50–100 critical parts.
Root 3 · Risk = probability × impact
P-04

A second source is only real if it is qualified and active

An alternative that has never received orders, has no tooling or was qualified on an old revision is only a name. Many chips have only one maker, so resilience often comes from design alternates and stock.

For us: keep alternates qualified by giving them some volume, and approve alternate parts at design.
Root 1 · Alternatives create leverage
P-05

Our own site is a single point of failure

We build everything at one site in New Zealand. An earthquake, fire, flood, power cut or IT failure there stops all production, whatever our suppliers do.

For us: include our own site in the resilience plan: continuity plan, insurance, backup power, IT recovery.
Root 3 · Risk = probability × impact
P-06

Buffers are insurance and must be priced like insurance

Strategic stock, capacity reservations and flexibility clauses all have a cost. The question is whether the cost is lower than probability × impact. Sometimes accepting the risk is right, if it is a written decision.

For us: compare the yearly cost of each buffer with the expected loss it prevents.
Root 2 · Total cost determines value
P-07

Response speed is designed in advance

Fast recovery comes from decisions made before the event: who decides, what they can spend and what the first steps are. In a shortage, the buyer who acts in the first hour wins supply.

For us: pre-approve spending limits for expediting and broker purchases in the playbook.
Root 4 · Influence is greatest before commitment

The Central Trade-off: Efficiency vs. Resilience

Every resilience measure costs money: stock, second sources, spare capacity, insurance. Too little leaves us exposed; too much wastes money on risks that are small. Fund resilience from a ranked list of exposures.

The system of rules and controls that makes procurement decisions authorised, defensible and consistent, and that protects both the company and its people.

At a Glance

Definition: Procurement governance is the set of policies, approval levels and controls that decide who can commit company money, how, and with what records.

Relevance to our business: In a small team at one site, one person often does several jobs, and people know suppliers personally. Clear rules protect the company and the individuals. Our products also cross borders, so trade compliance controls are part of governance.

  • Three layers: policy, procedure, work instruction.

First Principles

These principles explain why governance exists and how to make it work without slowing the business down.

P-01

Governance protects the individual as much as the organisation

A documented process, a clear approval and a declared conflict are what protect a buyer against an allegation. Working inside the rules is personal protection.

For us: if a decision is not recorded, assume it cannot be defended later.
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour
P-02

Authority must be explicit, delegated and bounded

Every commitment of company money should trace to a named authority level: who can approve what value, for which type of commitment. Unclear authority leads to unauthorised commitments made in good faith.

For us: keep a current delegation of authority matrix and apply it in the ERP system.
Root 7 · Incentives drive behaviour
P-03

Separation of duties is the core control

No single person should create a supplier, raise an order, receive the goods and approve the invoice. The 3-way match and separate requester and approver protect against fraud and honest error.

For us: in a small team where full separation is impossible, add compensating controls: second sign-off, rotation, sample audits.

P-04

Root 6 · Visibility precedes control

For us: log every exception centrally and review the pattern every quarter.
Root 6 · Visibility precedes control

For us: for any commitment, we can produce the full record within one day.

Core idea: six components that answer: what are the rules, who may commit, how is power separated, how is integrity protected, how do exceptions work, and how do we stay within trade law.

2 · Core Controls

Delegation of authority

A matrix of commitment type × value × role. Separate lines for purchases, contract signature, changes, disposals and exceptions. Authority belongs to the role, not the person. The total value of a commitment counts, not each instalment; splitting an order to stay under a limit is a breach.

Separation of duties

Incompatible pairs: creating a supplier vs. approving payment; requesting vs. approving; receiving vs. approving the invoice; recommending vs. approving your own recommendation. Where separation is impossible, document the compensating control.

Ethics and integrity

Gifts and hospitality register with limits and approval; conflict declarations every year and per sourcing event; anti-bribery rules including facilitation payments and agents; confidential handling of supplier prices; a safe way to raise concerns.

Exceptions and sole sources

A defined route for departures from standard process: written reason, approval one level above normal, time or value limits, central log. Sole-source reasons are dated and checked again at renewal.

3 · Law That Applies to Buyers

Competition law (NZ Commerce Act)

Cartel conduct (price fixing, market sharing, bid rigging) is a criminal offence in NZ, with up to 7 years in prison since 2021. Never share one supplier's prices with another. Report signs of suppliers coordinating their bids.

Unfair contract terms (NZ Fair Trading Act)

For standard-form business contracts up to NZ$250,000 per year ("small trade contracts"), unfair terms can be declared invalid. This matters when we use our standard terms with small NZ suppliers.

Export controls

Our own exports may need an MFAT permit if they are on the NZ Strategic Goods List (updated July 2026, with new electronics controls). Products with US-origin chips may also be subject to US export rules (EAR). Classify products and screen customers.

Sanctions

NZ sanctions under the Russia Sanctions Act 2022 and UN sanctions, plus US and EU sanctions that our customers and banks apply. Screen suppliers, customers and banks.

Spend under management (the share of spend through governed channels: contracts, catalogues, the purchase-order process) is the best single indicator of governance health. Low coverage means the rules exist on paper while money moves around them. Treat every persistent bypass as a design problem to fix.

Common Failure Modes

Governance fails in two directions: too little control invites loss; too much invites workarounds. Each failure violates a principle in tab 01.

01

Friction-driven bypass

Controls for routine purchases are so slow that people build workarounds: split card payments, invoices without orders, everything marked "urgent".

Violates P-04: controls must be proportionate.
Warning sign: rising share of invoices without a purchase order.
Measure cycle time for small purchases and cut it towards hours. Fix the path rather than policing the users.
02

Threshold splitting tolerated

Commitments are split into parts to stay under approval or tender limits.

Violates P-02: authority must be explicit and bounded.
Warning sign: several orders just below a threshold for the same supplier.
Define thresholds on total annual value. Run split-detection reports.
03

Sole-source approvals that never expire

"Only one supplier can do this" was written five years ago. The market now has three.

Violates P-06: exceptions must be visible.
Warning sign: sole-source approvals older than two years.
Give every sole-source approval an end date and check the market at renewal.
04

Conflicts not declared because "everyone knows"

In a small market, a known relationship is treated as declared. When a dispute arises, there is no record.

Violates P-05: conflicts are managed by declaration.
Warning sign: no declarations on file for recent sourcing events.
Declare yearly and per event, record centrally, and visibly remove the conflicted person.
05

Authority matrix out of date

After a reorganisation, the authority matrix describes roles that no longer exist.

Violates P-02: authority must be explicit.
Warning sign: ERP approval routes differ from the matrix.
Review the matrix after every restructure and every year.
06

Heavy control on small spend, none on large risk

Three quotes for a small purchase, while a large contract renews without competition.

Violates P-04: controls must be proportionate.
Warning sign: large renewals without a sourcing decision record.
Map control effort against spend and risk every year and move effort to where the risk is.
07

Competition law ignored

A buyer shares one supplier's price with another to "encourage" a better offer, or ignores signs of bid rigging.

Violates P-01: governance protects the individual.
Warning sign: supplier prices discussed with competitors.
Train buyers on the Commerce Act, keep bids confidential and report suspicious bid patterns.
08

Governance owned by no one

Policies written years ago, stored as documents, with no owner and no review.

Violates P-07: the audit trail is the deliverable.
Warning sign: policies with no review date.
Name an owner for each policy and publish the review calendar.

The Four Groups

E — departure (EXW): buyer does everything. F — main carriage unpaid (FCA/FAS/FOB): buyer arranges shipping. C — main carriage paid (CFR/CIF/CPT/CIP): seller pays freight but risk transfers at origin — the trap group. D — arrival (DAP/DPU/DDP): seller carries risk to destination.

Common Traps

FOB/CIF are maritime-only terms — for containers use FCA/CIP instead. CIF insurance is minimum cover (Institute Clauses C) — often inadequate for electronics; specify better or insure yourself. DDP makes the seller the importer of record — frequently impractical; DAP + buyer clears is usually cleaner. Under all C-terms, goods travel at your risk even though the seller paid the freight.

Formulas & Calculations

The working arithmetic. Each formula answers one commercial question — the variables matter more than the algebra.

Economic Order Quantity
EOQ = √( 2 × D × S ÷ H )
D = annual demand (units) · S = cost per order · H = holding cost per unit per year. Minimises ordering + carrying cost. Compare against supplier MOQs to price what the MOQ is costing you.
Safety Stock
SS = Z × σd × √LT
Z = service factor (90% → 1.28 · 95% → 1.65 · 98% → 2.05 · 99% → 2.33) · σd = std dev of demand per period · LT = lead time in periods. Scales linearly with Z, with the square root of lead time.
Reorder Point
ROP = (d̄ × LT) + SS
d̄ = average demand per period · LT = lead time in periods · SS = safety stock. Trigger on stock position (on hand + on order − allocated), never on-hand alone.
Inventory Turns
Turns = COGS ÷ Average Inventory
Higher = capital working harder. Days of inventory (DIO) = 365 ÷ turns. Watch the trend, not the absolute — turns vary hugely by industry.
Cash Conversion Cycle
CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO
DIO = days inventory · DSO = days sales outstanding · DPO = days payables outstanding = (AP ÷ COGS) × 365. Procurement moves DPO (terms) and DIO (inventory policy).
Payment Terms → Working Capital
Cash impact = Daily spend × Δdays
Daily spend = annual spend ÷ 365. Extending a NZD $10M supplier from 30 to 45 days releases ≈ $411k cash. Value the release at your cost of capital; weigh against any early-payment discount offered.
Early Payment Discount — Annualised
Rate ≈ d ÷ (100−d) × 365 ÷ (T − t)
d = discount % · t = discount window · T = standard terms. "2/10 net 30" ≈ 37% annualised — usually worth taking if cash allows. Compare against your cost of capital.
Purchase Price Variance
PPV = (Actual price − Std price) × Qty
Positive = we paid more than the standard (unfavourable). Useful as a tracking signal, dangerous as a target: PPV can be gamed by inflating standards or buying large quantities into stock.
Savings — Cost Reduction
Saving = (Baseline − New price) × Volume
Baseline = last price paid (like-for-like spec, terms, incoterm). Only P&L-visible with volume actually purchased. State baseline type explicitly; get finance sign-off on methodology once, then reuse.
Cost Avoidance
Avoidance = (Quoted/indexed increase − Settled) × Vol
Value of an increase resisted or specification saving vs. credible counterfactual. Real, but invisible in the P&L — always report separately from reduction, never blended.
Should-Cost Gap
Gap % = (Quote − Model) ÷ Model × 100
The negotiation agenda in one number. Decompose by cost layer (material / labour / overhead / margin) to locate where the conversation should focus.
Landed Cost
Unit + freight + duty + insurance + handling
Duty is calculated on customs value which may include freight/insurance depending on jurisdiction and incoterm. Compare suppliers only on landed cost at the same incoterm — never mixed terms.
